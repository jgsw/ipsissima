# 1 Introduction

<!-- p.1 begins here -->

The imagistic conception of thought, the conception of thinking as a form of imaging or picturing, is one of the recurring motifs in the history of philosophy and in psychology. Depending on the intellectual trend of an era, the association between thought and images has been more or less prevalent. The imageless thought controversy in the late 19th and early 20th century, for instance, illustrates how the idea of thought without image was something that required justification at the time (Ogden, 1911; Kunzendorf, 2021). On the other hand, the development of cognitive science and the conception of the mind as a computer operating on amodal symbols steered the trend in the opposite direction. This is illustrated by the so-called Imagery Debate in the 1980s and 90s, in which proponents of the imagistic view of thought found themselves needing to defend the claim that some kinds of thinking are inherently imagistic (Kosslyn, 1994; Pylyshyn, 1981).

The history of Western philosophy features several major thinkers who endorsed the imagistic conception. This paper will focus on the Early Modern era, when canonical authors such as Berkeley and Hume advanced imagistic accounts of the nature of thought.[^1] Their views stemmed from the empiricist assumption that the foundation of thought and knowledge consists of elements derived from sensory perception. More complex forms of cognition, they argued, are ultimately composed of these elements. The technical term used by Berkeley and Hume to refer to these basic components is ‘idea’. While Berkeley applies it to the components of sensory perception as well, Hume reserves the term ‘idea’ for the elements of thinking that are distinct from direct sensory perception.

From the present-day perspective, quasi-perceptual elements of thought, distinct from the elements of direct sensory perception, are typically described as ‘mental imagery’.[^2] We define mental imagery as the tokening of a quasi-perceptual representation that is generated endogenously rather than by incoming sensory signals. The Early Modern empiricist conception of ideas and the contemporary notion of mental imagery can therefore be understood as referring to the same psychological phenomenon. Both have a nature that cannot be captured in an amodal and abstract way, but rather in a mode through which the object of thought is given to the subject as something sensible and specific.

If thinking requires mental imagery, as philosophers like Hume and (early) Berkeley appear to assume, what would they say about agents who lack mental imagery? Would they say that such agents are in fact not thinking beings at all, given that they do not satisfy one of the preconditions of thought? Prima facie, the commitments of these philosophers suggest an affirmative answer. Yet in that case, they would face difficulties in making sense of the existence of agents who can think without using mental imagery.

<!-- p.2 begins here -->

What has become increasingly plausible in recent years is that such agents do exist. Take aphantasia, an apparent inability to generate mental imagery. Although subject to ongoing theoretical and empirical debates over its psychological details, the condition itself is now well- established. While aphantasics are clearly agents who think, they do not seem to satisfy the conditions for thought that were posited by the Early Modern empiricist philosophers.

This tension between Humean conception of abstraction and aphantasia deserves to be scrutinized further, and that is exactly what we intend to do in this paper. In particular, we are going to examine how Hume would respond to this problem. We chose Hume because, among prominent empiricist philosophers, he developed one of the most elaborate accounts of how thought can be grounded in images (see Allison, 2008: 7). Examining how Hume could accommodate aphantasia in his account of thinking thus provides an instructive test case for assessing the capacity of full-blown empiricist theories of the mind to accommodate aphantasia.[^3]

More precisely, this paper focuses on Hume's account of abstraction, i.e., his explanation of how the mind forms abstract ideas such as the idea of a triangle or the idea of friendship. Hume's account of abstraction is one of the most central parts of his theory where he tries to reconcile his imagistic commitments with the fact that most people are able to think about abstract matters that do not seem to involve a sensuous mode of presentation. It is thus one of the most salient points at which Hume tries to offer an explanation of how thought in general is grounded in images. While Hume's attempt is admirable, we will argue that aphantasia nevertheless poses a problem for his view of abstraction.[^4]

Our argument also has broader implications for how we should understand the place of abstract thought in human cognition in light of aphantasia.

As far as we know, implications of aphantasia on understanding the genesis of abstract thought have not yet been explored in contemporary research on concepts and language comprehension. This is a significant gap, however, given that there are theories in that field that are predicated on the manipulation of off-line sensory and perceptual representations in grounding conceptual thought (e.g., Barsalou, 1999, 2008; Meteyard et al., 2012). Aphantasics as subjects who, as we will argue, do not operate with such representations but are capable of abstract thought pose a prima facie challenge to such theories. The analysis of how Hume could explain aphantasics’ capacity for abstract thought is instructive for understanding the structure of that challenge.

Furthermore, the argument presented in this paper is clearly relevant to contemporary theories of concepts that are explicitly inspired by the views of Early Modern empiricist philosophers. For instance, the kind of abstraction mechanism that Hume appealed to can be found in Jesse Prinz's theory of concepts. Prinz takes concepts to be what he calls ‘proxytypes’, which are endogenously controlled perceptual representations that are supposed to represent other instances that fall under their scope (Prinz 2004, 197). If the argument in this paper against the Humean account of abstraction has any bite, it also has it against Prinz's view.[^5]

Aside from having implications for the contemporary theories of concepts, the argument in this paper has potential repercussions for how to understand and study mental imagery and aphantasia empirically. In particular, as we lay out our argument, we will introduce a distinction between the mere possession of mental imagery and the imaginative uses of mental imagery.[^6] Furthermore, among those uses, we identify a specific kind of imaginative use that is relevant for Humean abstraction. As we argue, evidence for the presence of mental imagery does not by itself establish that such imagery is being used imaginatively, nor does evidence of imaginative use suffice to show that imagery is used in the way required for Humean abstraction. If our argument is on the right track, empirical study of aphantasia should be sensitive to such nuances (we explain this in more detail in sections 2.2 and 4.1).

The structure of the paper is as follows. In the next section, we will describe Hume's account of how abstract ideas are formed and how this account relates to the concepts of mental imagery and imagining in the contemporary sense. Then, in Section 3, we will describe current research on aphantasia in more detail and argue that aphantasia poses a challenge to Hume's account of abstraction. Section 4 will consider different ways of responding to that challenge, none of which, in our view, will succeed.

# 2 Hume and abstraction

This section aims to provide an overview of Hume's theory of the mind, focusing specifically on his account of how individual (or particular)[^7] ideas become general through abstraction. We will also explain how his concept of individual ideas and the description of the process of abstraction relate to the notions of mental imagery and imagining as discussed in contemporary literature. This explanation is necessary because in order to demonstrate that aphantasia poses a challenge to Hume's account of abstraction, we must also show that Hume's framework for understanding the mind concerns the same psychological phenomena that are posited in contemporary research on mental imagery and aphantasia. In addition, in introducing Hume's account of abstraction, we also specify the kind of imagination that is required for abstraction.

# 2.1 Individual ideas and imagination

In Book 1 of the Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40), Hume famously describes all contents of the human mind as perceptions, dividing these into two categories: impressions and ideas. The former include “sensations, passions and emotions” and differ from the latter by a greater degree of “force and liveliness.” Ideas are “faint images” of the impressions “in thinking and reasoning” (T 1.1.1.1). He further distinguishes between simple and complex impressions and ideas, explaining complexity in terms of being distinguishable into parts (T 1.1.1.2). Impressions are causes of ideas, as when a sensation of a particular color or smell produces corresponding simple ideas in the mind (T 1.1.1.8). He establishes it as a general rule that there is a relation of resemblance between simple ideas and simple impressions (T 1.1.1.5–6). This leads to the proposition commonly known as Hume's Copy Principle: “[A]ll our simple ideas in their first appearance are deriv'd from simple impressions, which are correspondent to them, and which they exactly represent” (T 1.1.1.7; see Garrett, 1997: 41–57).

In Hume's view, thinking requires having ideas in the sense that to think about something is to form an idea that represents it (Cottrell, 2015: § 1). Ideas are all individual by nature but, as will be explained below, they can become general by being made to represent other ideas of the same kind. Individual ideas are literally image-like (T 1.1.7.10; cf. Garrett, 2008: 50) and can be identified with mental imagery in the contemporary sense. There are sufficient phenomenological and representational similarities between individual ideas and mental imagery to support this equation. Hume's individual ideas have sensory features and appear to agents as perceptual or quasi-perceptual. The same applies to mental imagery (Dijkstra et al., 2019: 423). What it’s like to perceive X is at least partially reflected in the experience of having a mental image of X. Furthermore, similar to Hume's individual ideas, mental imagery can be characterized as more or less vivid, clear and detailed.

<!-- p.3 begins here -->

For Hume, thinking is the work of imagination taken in a broad sense that includes reason but excludes memory.[^8] The ideas of memory and imagination differ in their degree of vivacity: the former are “much more lively and strong” than the latter (T 1.1.3.1; 1.3.5.5). They also differ in that “the imagination is not restrain'd to the same order and form with the original impressions; while the memory is in a manner ty'd down in that respect, without any power of variation” (T 1.1.3.2). Unlike memory, imagination is free “to transpose and change its ideas” (T 1.1.3.4), implying at least some control over these activities (see Dorsch, 2016: 47; 2018: 11). This freedom of the imagination to combine and rearrange ideas bears a functional similarity to how imagistic imagination (or sensory imagination or perceptual imagination)[^9] is understood in contemporary literature. Imagistic imagination entails the freedom to recombine mental images (e.g. Van Leeuwen, 2013; Gregory, 2016) and is also to some extent under the agent's control (e.g., Arcangeli, 2018; Goldwasser, 2024; Sant'anna 2023). Accordingly, we take it that imaginative operation with individual ideas, as Hume describes it, refers to imagining by using mental imagery or imagistic imagining in the contemporary sense.

Before moving on to describe Hume's theory of abstraction, we need to address a possible objection that concerns our identification of Hume's notion of individual ideas with mental imagery. It could be objected that there are significant differences between them. In light of contemporary empirical research, it could be argued that mental imagery is a neuroscientific category that is essentially defined in terms of neurocognitive processes (compare Nanay, 2023). Hume's notion of individual ideas, on the other hand, has nothing to do with neuroscientific categories, and is instead understood in phenomenal and representational terms.

As a response, it should suffice to point out that mental imagery is not merely a neuroscientific category. Even if its causal basis consists in neural processes, there are still sufficient phenomenological and representational similarities between mental images and Hume's individual ideas to conclude that the two are identical. Arguably, there is no other contemporary psychological category that comes even close. If it turned out that the contemporary category of mental imagery cannot be identified with Hume's notion, this would render his account of abstraction immediately hopeless from the contemporary perspective.

It is helpful here to think of mental imagery in terms of the homeostatic property cluster theory of natural kinds (Boyd, 1999), according to which natural kinds are clusters of properties that tend to be co-instantiated due to some mechanism that constitutes the causal basis of that clustering. On this view, understanding the properties within the cluster remains relevant for understanding the natural kind in question, even if its “essence” lies elsewhere. Applied to mental imagery, this perspective implies that the characteristic phenomenological and representational features of mental imagery are important for understanding it as a natural kind, even if its causal basis is rooted in something neurophysiological or neuropsychological. This view also allows for the identification of individual ideas with mental imagery because, although Hume lacked knowledge of modern neuroscience, he attributed to individual ideas many of the features that we now associate with the property cluster that characterizes mental imagery.[^10]

# 2.2 Abstraction

Let's now turn to Hume's account of abstraction. Hume's account begins by adopting Berkeley's view that all ideas are individual in nature and can only be general by signifying a number of other individual ideas of the same kind (T 1.1.7.1).[^11] Both philosophers think that general ideas exist, but they are essentially individual ideas representing other individual ideas of the same kind. While Berkeley rejects even the term ‘abstract idea,’ Hume uses it interchangeably with ‘general idea’:

Abstract ideas are therefore in themselves individual, however they may become general in their representation. The image in the mind is only that of a particular object, tho’ the application of it in our reasoning be the same, as if it were universal. (T 1.1.7.6)

What is missing from Berkeley's account is a psychological mechanism that explains how “mental particulars are able to function as universals when connected with general terms” (Allison, 2008: 30; cf. Bradshaw, 1988). Hume introduces such a mechanism, relying on the observation that the human mind associates similar ideas and applies general terms to them. When confronted with a general term, the mind uses imagination to conjure up one of the individual ideas that the term stands for:

When we have found a resemblance among several objects, that often occur to us, we apply the same name to all of them, whatever differences we may observe in the degrees of their quantity and quality, and whatever other differences may appear among them.

After we have acquired a custom of this kind, the hearing of that name revives the idea of one of these objects, and makes the imagination conceive it with all its particular circumstances and proportions. (T 1.1.7.7)

While the mind can consider certain individual ideas as representatives of other ideas of the same kind, it typically cannot entertain all of those other ideas. One cannot imaginatively represent every triangle or every instance of blueness. When generality is conferred to an individual idea, the other individual ideas that it represents: are not really and in fact present to the mind, but only in power; nor do we draw them all out distinctly in the imagination, but keep ourselves in a readiness to survey any of them, as we may be prompted by a present design or necessity. The word raises up an individual idea, along with a certain custom; and that custom produces any other individual one, for which we may have occasion. But as the production of all the ideas, to which the name may be apply’d, is in most cases impossible, we abridge that work by a more partial consideration, and find but few inconveniencies to arise in our reasoning from that abridgment. (Ibid.)

<!-- p.4 begins here -->

The actual grasp of generality is only partial. What is required is the readiness of the mind to imagine any of the individual ideas that fall under the general term. The crux of the psychological mechanism enabling abstraction consists in the custom of the mind to conjure those individual ideas when presented with the terms. Hume illustrates this through the example of the general term ‘triangle,’ which is connected with a variety of ideas of triangles with different shapes and sizes. The mind has developed a custom of invoking these individual ideas in the imagination when hearing or reading the term. While the mind cannot entertain all the ideas signified by the term ‘triangle,’ it can conjure some of them and is ready to consider any of them. The partial grasp of the scope of ideas signified by the term is sufficient for the mind to be able to think about triangles in general (T 1.1.7.8-10; see also Broughton, 2000: 280–282).

The core of Hume's account of abstraction can be summarized in contemporary terms as follows. The psychological mechanism responsible for abstraction is a kind of projective imagination that involves invoking some determinate mental image and using it to call to mind other mental images that belong under the same category. Projective imagination involves internally driven generation of multiple images, a process that is sensitive to category-relevant similarities between them. This sensitivity requires selective attention to the features that generalize across category members (e.g. being triangular) while ignoring the features that are not relevant to the category (e.g. the color or size of the triangle). Furthermore, one also needs to filter out irrelevant images that the mind might spontaneously produce (e.g. an image of a square). This way of using imagination is thus different from merely having mental imagery. It is also more specific than imagination in the general sense of controlled manipulation of mental images in that not all imaginings entail the projective capacity required for abstraction.

Hume's imagistic framework raises a significant question: can individuals who lack the capacity to imaginatively operate with mental imagery still perform abstraction in a Humean sense? Since Hume maintains that abstraction requires that capacity, his account faces a challenge in explaining how agents who do not have it can nonetheless engage in abstract thought. The following section will examine aphantasics as such agents.

# 3 Challenge from aphantasia

In this section, we will state the prima facie challenge from aphantasia to Hume's view of abstraction. But first, we will take a closer look at aphantasia and give an overview of its main features that are relevant for assessing Hume's view of abstraction. We will argue that the crucial feature of aphantasia in the present context is the apparent inability of a subset of aphantasics to imagine by using mental imagery. Insofar as at least some aphantasics have this deficit, it is possible to argue that Hume's account of abstraction faces difficulties in trying to account for how aphantasics could form abstract ideas.

The term ‘aphantasia’ was first used in the study by Zeman et al. (2015) to denote participants' self-reported visual imagery deficits. Aphantasics are subjects who, when asked to visualize some scenario, report that they are unable to do that. Some aphantasics even report surprise after having realized that other people literally see quasi-perceptual images in their mind's eye, thinking that the talk about mental imagery was merely metaphorical (Korot 2022).

Aphantasia as an imagery deficit was already noted by Galton (1880) who described people with an inability to visualize. After the popularization by Zeman,[^12] however, aphantasia is now more systematically investigated by psychologists and neuroscientists (e.g., Keogh et al., 2021; Argueta et al., 2025). There are some relatively uncontroversial claims that can be made in light of those investigations. First, aphantasia comes in both congenital and acquired forms (our focus will be on the congenital form). Second, aphantasia is not just a visual imagery impairment but concerns other sensory modalities as well (Dawes et al., 2020).

Aphantasia has been typically investigated by relying on subjective self-report measures, such as the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ) (Marks, 1973). In VVIQ, people are asked questions that require visualizing 16 prompts and evaluating the vividness and clarity of their mental images on a 5-point Likert scale. The highest score is 80 (each image receives the highest vividness rating) and the lowest is 16 (each image receives the lowest rating). Analogous questionnaires have been developed also for other imagery modalities (Andrade et al., 2014; Croijmans et al., 2019). As it is usually a case with questionnaires that target introspective reports, the VVIQ has received its share of criticism as a robust measure (e.g., Runge et al., 2015; Kind, 2017).

There are now also other, less subjective neurophysiological and behavioral differences associated with aphantasia, however. For instance, neuroimaging, mainly fMRI,[^13] has been used to identify the brain areas whose activity is relevant for distinguishing between aphantasics and non-aphantasics (e.g., Pearson, 2019; Chang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Monzel et al., 2024).[^14] There are also behavioral indicators associated with aphantasia. For instance, it is well-established that vivid mental images tend to bias which stimulus image dominates in the case of binocular rivalry (Pearson et al., 2011; Pearson, 2014). When this effect is reduced or absent, it serves as an indicator of aphantasia (Keogh and Pearson, 2018, 2024).

Aphantasics are at the one end of a spectrum where at the other end are hyperphantasics: those individuals who report extremely high degree of imagery vividness. It is by now quite clear that a significant number of people lie on the aphantasic end of the spectrum. For instance, some data suggest that 4% of the human population are aphantasics (Dance et al., 2022), while people with lifelong severe aphantasia are estimated to make up approximately 1% of the human population (Zeman, 2024). In what follows, we will focus on severe aphantasia in particular, in contrast with moderate aphantasia. Severe aphantasics are individuals who, as indicated by their self-reports, are unable to form mental imagery, at least insofar as conscious imagery is concerned.[^15] This is reflected in their VVIQ scores, which are at the minimum possible value (16 out of 80), whereas moderate aphantasics score higher.

What aphantasia exactly consists of is up for debate. For example, Phillips (2026: 70) provides a helpful distinction between the following four types of accounts. According to the first, aphantasia is due to idiosyncratic reporting. According to the second, aphantasia involves faulty introspection (defended by Schwitzgebel, 2011 [^16]; see also Nanay, 2021). According to the third, aphantasics lack conscious imagery but have intact unconscious imagery (see Michel et al., 2025).[^17] According to the fourth, aphantasics have no imagery (see Keogh and Pearson, 2018).[^18] Other interpretations have proposed that aphantasia involves a deficit of the episodic construction system (Blomkvist, 2023), that it concerns voluntary imagination in particular (Zeman et al., 2015), and that aphantasics lack object imagery but have spatial imagery (Phillips’ own view). We will address some of the controversies concerning the nature of aphantasia in the next section.

<!-- p.5 begins here -->

It should be clear by now that there is at least a tension between Hume's view of abstraction and the existence of individuals with severe aphantasia. This tension suffices to present a prima facie challenge to his view. Recall that for Hume, abstraction requires imagining by using mental images. Severe aphantasics, however, fail to respond to the invitation to visualize, i.e., to imagine by means of visual mental imagery, which suggests that they are incapable of abstraction in the Humean sense. The implication that aphantasics are unable to engage in abstraction would be difficult to stomach: aphantasics as cognitively functioning individuals are capable of abstract thought as much as anyone else. This is evident both in everyday situations and in experimental contexts where aphantasics are involved as competent conversational partners and articulate their thoughts about abstract matters. In view of this tension, Hume's imagistic account of abstraction faces the explanatory challenge of accommodating the ability of aphantasics to engage in abstract thought.[^19]

# 4 Possible responses

In what follows, we will consider four possible responses to the challenge we have presented and argue why they won't succeed. The first two are primarily from the perspective of contemporary empirical research; the other two are more exegetically based.

There are some additional options that deserve to be mentioned, although we won't explore them in detail.

First, someone could try to argue that since severe aphantasics have intact involuntary mental images (Krempel and Monzel, 2024; K¨onigsmark et al., 2021), they could perform Humean abstraction by using involuntary mental imagery. This proposal is not workable because in Hume, imagination is under at least some control of the agent, i.e., it is not entirely involuntary.

Second, someone could try to argue that severe aphantasics could perform Humean abstraction by using memory, instead of imagination. This proposal is not workable because in Hume, memory preserves the previously experienced order and position of past experiences and it is arguably the lack of such constraints that makes imagination the suitable candidate to play the abstractive role.

Third, someone could try to argue that aphantasics can perform Humean abstraction by means of mental imagery from non-visual sensory modalities. Since the main focus of aphantasia research has been on visual mental imagery (or the lack thereof), a case could be made that aphantasics have imagery of other modalities intact, by which they could perform abstraction. This proposal is also problematic, however. As we already pointed out, there are reasons for thinking that there is also multisensory aphantasia. As an illustration, consider the recent study by Delem et al. (2025).[^20] They conducted a clustering analysis that suggested three cognitive profiles of individuals who performed various behavioral tasks. One of the clusters included aphantasics who relied predominantly on verbal strategies while having low imagery across all sensory modalities (see also Takahashi et al., 2023). Aphantasia thus is not limited to visual sensory imagery impairment and the proponent of Humean view of abstraction is not safe to assume that aphantasics can perform abstraction by using imagery from other sensory modalities.

# 4.1 Unconscious imagery in aphantasia?

As we mentioned in the previous section, one of the controversies surrounding aphantasia is whether aphantasics have unconscious imagery. Since subjective measures are not suitable for probing unconscious imagery and much of the evidence for aphantasia is based on such measures, there is room to argue that aphantasics only lack conscious imagery, but not unconscious imagery (Nanay, 2023; Michel et al., 2025). In this subsection, we examine whether aphantasics might perform abstraction via unconscious imagery.[^21]

Some empirical studies suggest that aphantasics do, in fact, have mental imagery, although they are not conscious of it themselves. For instance, Siena and Simons (2024), tested aphantasics' performance in 3D object and spatial memory tasks where the participants had to reproduce the location and the color hue of the remembered object, while the recall perspective was manipulated. As it turned out, aphantasics weren't impaired in any of the subjective or objective measures, suggesting that they had their imagery intact. Also, Liu & Bartolomeo (2023) had aphantasic and non-aphantasic participants performing imagery and perception tasks in which they judged which of the two items better matched a heard attribute, either by forming mental images of the items (imagery task) or by seeing the items (perception task). While aphantasic participants responded more slowly on both types of tasks, their accuracy was comparable to that of controls, suggesting that although aphantasics process visual information more slowly, they have intact imagistic representations.

Neural data have also been interpreted as showing that aphantasics possess intact but unconscious mental imagery. For instance, a fMRI study by Liu et al. (2025) has shown that domain-specific ventral temporal cortex regions and the domain-general fusiform imagery node still activate during imagery tasks despite the absence of conscious visual experience. This suggests that image-like representations can occur beneath the threshold of consciousness. Also, Milton et al. (2021) had participants either view or imagine famous faces and places, and despite aphantasics reporting no visual imagery, their visual cortex activation during the imagery task was not reduced compared to controls, suggesting intact imagistic representations.

We are doubtful, however, if the appeal to unconscious imagery can settle that severe aphantasics can in fact satisfy the conditions of Humean abstraction. There are two reasons for this. First, there is contrary evidence to the effect that at least some aphantasics do not have any imagery whatsoever. Second, given that Humean abstraction requires a specific kind of imaginative use of imagery, it is implausible that severe aphantasics satisfy this requirement, even if they have unconscious imagery. Either way, the explanatory challenge to account for how severe aphantasics can perform Humean abstraction still stands.

Let's consider the first reason in more detail. There is significant behavioral and physiological evidence for the view that aphantasics lack unconscious imagery. In a recent study, Purkart et al. (2025) designed an implicit priming task where participants had to indicate the orientation of a Gabor patch while having been previously presented with a red or blue circle. The circle was supposed to activate an unconscious mental image of a Gabor patch that they were exposed to during the previous association phase. In the explicit task, they had to explicitly form a mental image of a previously experienced patch. The idea was that if aphantasics had intact unconscious imagery, they should exhibit the priming effect in the implicit task where unconscious imagery would have an effect on their performance. As it turned out, however, aphantasics exhibited priming effects neither in the explicit nor in the implicit task, suggesting the absence of both conscious and unconscious imagery (see also Keogh and Pearson, 2018). In addition to this study, it has been also shown that aphantasics have reduced automatic pupil dilatation in response to imagined bright stimuli (Kay et al., 2022) and reduced skin conductance response to reading frightening stories (Wicken et al., 2021). Since both dilatation and skin conductance response are generally associated with the activation of mental imagery, these data further support the idea that aphantasics may lack any imagery, whether conscious or unconscious.

<!-- p.6 begins here -->

Second, some neural evidence also speaks against the existence of intact imagery in aphantasia. For instance, by analyzing BOLD signals in the primary visual cortex, Chang et al. (2025) demonstrated that imagistic content could be decoded from the early visual cortex of both typical imagers and aphantasics. What was significant was that unlike the control subjects, aphantasics' neural representations in the early visual cortex during imagery tasks could not be cross-decoded with their neural representations that were activated during perception. This suggests that the visual system encodes task-relevant mental representations in both groups, but not in the same format as in the case of perception. This motivated the authors to coin the term ‘imageless imagery’ to describe aphantasics' mental representations. However, if the way in which ‘normal’ imagery represents is significantly different from the way in which the putative imagistic representations of aphantasics represent, it is unclear if the latter counts as mental imagery in a meaningful sense. As McKilliam and Kirberg (2025) point out, the neural activity observed in early visual areas of aphantasics may be too impoverished or too indirect to qualify as genuinely imagistic (for some further discussion, see Scholz, 2025; Scholz et al., 2025). The failure of cross-decoding thus suggests that aphantasics do not have intact quasi-perceptual representations.

We can now turn to the second reason why appealing to unconscious imagery in aphantasia does not successfully address our challenge to Humean abstraction. It is crucial to note that the challenge does not rest on the assumption that aphantasics lack mental images altogether, but rather on the claim that they cannot imagine by using mental imagery in the sense relevant for abstraction. The key issue, then, is not whether aphantasics exhibit imagery-like neural patterns or can perform certain imagistic tasks, but whether aphantasics can use the representations underlying these patterns in the way that Humean abstraction requires. That is, can aphantasics select a mental image to represent a range of other images that are instances of the relevant category, and also be disposed to project it to all other instances? This remains unproven. We need not even deny that aphantasics possess internal representations that could broadly be described as imagery; what matters is whether these representations can sustain the kind of projective imagining required for abstraction. Having and manipulating a mental image of a triangle is one thing; being able to use that image to project it onto other triangles is quite another.

That aphantasics do not have that capacity is also in line with some recent evidence suggesting that aphantasia involves a difficulty with activating the relevant top-down processes by which mental images are generated and manipulated.[^22] In particular, Boere et al. (2025) report EEG evidence which indicates that aphantasics exhibit reduced frontal delta power that is crucial for filtering out irrelevant information and maintaining the manipulation of internally generated representations. Since, as we argued in 2.2, such filtering is required for projective imagination, this study provides a preliminary reason to suspect that aphantasics fail at projective imagination.[^23] We acknowledge that citing one study is insufficient to prove that aphantasics are incapable of projective imagination. Our concerns should be read as an invitation for empirical researchers to develop more robust experimental paradigms to test whether aphantasics are capable of projective imagination.

In conclusion, in light of the available empirical evidence, it is implausible that aphantasics can perform Humean abstraction by using unconscious mental imagery. At most, the evidence supports the existence of mental representations in aphantasics which fall short of playing the required cognitive role. Until it is shown that such representations can be deployed in the requisite imaginative manner, the claim that aphantasics can perform Humean abstraction by relying on intact but unconscious imagery remains unsubstantiated.

# 4.2 Spatial imagery

Another response appeals to the dissociation between different kinds of mental imagery. The most notable distinction in the present context is between object imagery and spatial imagery (Blazhenkova et al., 2006; see also Farah et al., 1988). Object imagery is a mental representation of the sensory features of an object. For instance, visual object imagery concerns the visual features of an object such as the shape, brightness and color. Spatial imagery, on the other hand, is a mental representation of spatial locations of objects and relations between them. While object imagery has the phenomenology that resembles the phenomenology of perception, the feeling as if one were perceiving objects, the phenomenology of spatial imagery is more opaque. It is an open question if spatial imagery is unconscious, but we do not need to take a stand on this question in the present context.

A case can be made that severe aphantasics, despite lacking object imagery, still retain spatial imagery. Given that they retain spatial imagery they can arguably draw on spatial imagery to perform various cognitive tasks, suggesting that object imagery is not needed for those tasks. For instance, as a recent study indicates, aphantasics often use spatial (and sensorimotor) strategies to solve visual working memory tasks (Reeder et al., 2024). Mental rotation that relies on the understanding of how spatial relations change under transformations is a task that is perfectly suited for spatial imagery. This gives some reason to think that aphantasics have intact spatial imagery abilities (Phillips, 2026). In addition, since aphantasics appear to have above average scores in the spatial aspect of the Object and Spatial Imagery Questionnaire (Keogh and Pearson, 2018),[^24] Since the differences in spatial imagery between aphantasics and normal imagers appear to be considerably less pronounced than differences in object imagery between them.

If severe aphantasics have intact spatial imagery that can be used for various purposes, could it also be relied on to perform Humean abstraction? If Humean abstraction requires forming a custom to imagine a range of individual ideas that fall under a general term in response to a single individual idea associated with the term, couldn't severe aphantasics just perform abstraction by forming a custom to imagine a range of spatial images?

<!-- p.7 begins here -->

The first problem with this response is that it is unclear how severe aphantasics with intact spatial imagery could exactly perform Humean abstraction. Since spatial imagery is a representation of spatial relations but not modality-specific properties, spatial imagery does not seem suitable for representing determinate sensible particulars. However, Hume's view assumes the need for such representations. Individual ideas in Hume's sense, such as the ideas “of scarlet or orange, of sweet or bitter”, have modally specific sensory features which are given by object imagery, not spatial imagery (T 1.1.1.8). To put it in another way, while individual ideas in Hume have sensory content, spatial imagery is non- sensory.

A second issue is that spatial aphantasia may also exist, that is, some people may be incapable of forming spatial imagery. Evidence for this comes from Palermo et al. (2022), in which participants completed the Object and Spatial Imagery Questionnaire. Around 3.5% of respondents scored very low on the spatial imagery items, suggesting that spatial aphantasia is a real phenomenon. This possibility was already hinted at by a 1995 case study of a patient with impaired spatial imagery but intact visual imagery (Morton and Morris, 1995). If spatial aphantasia exists, then a parallel argument can be constructed against a version of Hume's account of abstraction that grounds abstraction in spatial rather than object imagery. Since such a version relies on spatial imagery, it faces the challenge of explaining how individuals with spatial aphantasia are capable of abstraction.

# 4.3 Abstraction by using words as proxies

As explained earlier, language plays an important role in the formation of abstract ideas, according to Hume. General terms invoke individual ideas connected to them and enable the mind to regard an individual idea as representing all ideas of its kind. One could possibly extend the role of language even further and argue that individuals with aphantasia are able to mimic abstraction by using operations with words as proxies for mental imagery. Hume definitely states that in reasoning, which is analogous to abstracting, “we do not annex distinct and compleat ideas to every term we make use of” (T 1.1.7.14; our italics). One could argue, in a more radical manner, that aphantasics do not use any ideas or imagery in the processes of abstracting and reasoning, instead relying solely on linguistic operations. This is also in accordance with the evidence, cited above, that aphantasics rely on verbal strategies to perform various behavioral tasks.

An obvious problem with this response is that, as explained earlier, Hume's understanding of how human thinking works is essentially imagistic. In other words, the ideational component is necessary for forming abstract ideas that, in turn, enable the mind to think about general matters and engage in sophisticated reasoning. While the operational use of words is an integral part of the process of abstraction, the process, as described by Hume, is inevitably concerned with individual ideas. These are ideas or images that severe aphantasics are apparently incapable of producing and entertaining. Additionally, this response suggests that individuals with severe aphantasia think and speak using general terms whose meanings they do not grasp. While there are debates on the details of Hume's concept of linguistic meaning, it seems clear that for him, using words without any associated ideas would be equivalent to thinking and speaking nonsense (see Ott, 2006).

# 4.4 Aphantasia as mere exception

Finally, we need to consider a slightly different reaction to the challenge from aphantasia. It could be argued that although the discovery of severe aphantasia means that Hume's account of abstraction is not applicable to severe aphantasics, this does not yet mean that we should give up the theory full-stop. According to this response, Hume could just treat aphantasia as an interesting exception. Given that severe aphantasics constitute a small minority in the human population, Hume might not have to treat them as constituting a counterexample that would require giving up his account of abstraction. From the fact that a theory of abstraction does not explain all the cases of abstraction, we should not conclude that the theory isn't fruitful, insofar as it still makes sense of most cases. This response is quite different from the rest because it lets Hume maintain that his account of abstraction is a good explanation of typical cases.

This response raises a broader metatheoretical question of how we should treat counterexamples in the context of philosophical theorizing. Since addressing this question is not within the scope of this paper, we focus on whether Hume, given his commitments, could treat severe aphantasia as an exception that calls for a different explanation from his basic account that appeals to imaginative operations with individual ideas.

It is highly implausible that he could. As described in Section 2, one of the foundational assumptions in Hume's conception of the mind is the Copy Principle according to which all simple ideas are copies of corresponding impressions. This means that all the basic building blocks of thought are imagistic in nature. By postulating that there is a kind of abstractive process that does not presuppose imagistic operation with individual ideas implies that not all abstractive mental operations have individual ideas as their objects. This is inconsistent with the Copy Principle because the latter implies that ideas inherit their particular nature from the impressions that they are copies of.[^25]

[S]ince all impressions are clear and precise, the ideas, which are copy’d from them, must be of the same nature, and can never, but from our fault, contain any thing so dark and intricate. (T 1.3.1.7)

Because the Copy Principle is supposed to be a universal thesis, it is unclear what theoretical space there is for Hume to allow for mental operations that do not operate with individual ideas. But this is exactly what he needs if he wants to argue for an alternative conception of abstraction that is suitable to explain the abstract thought of severe aphantasics. By giving up the Copy Principle, the resulting account of the mind would cease to be Hume's account in any meaningful sense.

It is instructive to consider a comparison with a case that Hume himself acknowledged as a counterexample to his theory of the mind and the Copy Principle in particular: the missing shade of blue. The case goes as follows: suppose someone has experienced every shade of blue except one. Hume allows that it is conceivable that this person, upon seeing all the other shades arranged in a sequence, could mentally fill in the gap and form an idea of the missing shade, even though they have never had a corresponding sensory impression. This seems to violate the Copy Principle, which holds that all simple ideas must derive from antecedent impressions. However, Hume treats this as a singular and minor exception, so rare and unimportant, in his view, that it does not justify revising or rejecting the general principle (T 1.1.1.10; EHU 2.8).

As Earp (2020) argues, Hume can regard the missing shade of blue as a legitimate counterexample yet still retain the broader theory by treating the case as an isolated anomaly—an exception that does not undermine the explanatory power of the overall framework. However, the missing shade of blue case is quite different from the challenge posed by aphantasia. In the case of the former, Hume can plausibly treat the phenomenon as a minor curiosity, i.e., an exceptional case so narrow and contained that it poses no serious challenge to the theory's explanatory reach. In contrast, aphantasia raises a more serious concern. It is not plausible that the capacity for abstraction in individuals with severe aphantasia can be explained through entirely separate cognitive mechanisms from those employed by typical individuals. Because the capacity for abstract conceptual thinking underlies most, if not all, cognitive processes, positing a special abstraction mechanism for aphantasics would imply that most, if not all, of their cognitive mechanisms differ from those of non-aphantasics. By contrast, proposing a mechanism to explain the missing shade of blue case would not entail such far-reaching consequences for the cognitive architecture of aphantasics because the capacity needed to fill in a missing impression does not underlie cognition in general.

<!-- p.8 begins here -->

The challenge is therefore not merely one of accommodating an exception but of explaining how abstraction is even possible without reliance on the imagistic processes that Hume sees as essential. On the other hand, if Hume were to remain satisfied with his theory of abstraction as it stands—as he does in the case of the Copy Principle despite the challenge posed by the missing shade of blue—this would commit him to the view that individuals with severe aphantasia are incapable of abstract thought. Such a conclusion is not only implausible; it verges on the offensive. It would entail that severe aphantasics should be treated as devoid of reason.

# 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we argued that severe aphantasia poses a challenge to Hume's view of abstraction that explains it in terms of the capacity to imagine using mental imagery. Since individuals with severe aphantasia appear to lack that capacity while nevertheless engaging in abstract thought, this raises the question of how Hume's account of abstraction can accommodate such cases. We examined potential empirical and exegetical responses to this challenge and argued that none of them has successfully addressed it.

The arguments presented in this paper hold a wider significance beyond just evaluating Hume's account of abstraction. The existence of severe aphantasia may also pose problems for other historical and contemporary theories that try to ground various higher-level mental phenomena in the capacity to generate mental imagery. Furthermore, the challenge that we proposed makes salient how such theories need to be sensitive to specific kinds of operations, such as projective imagination, that can be performed with mental imagery. Severe aphantasia thus serves not merely as a counterexample to particular theories, but as a productive constraint on future accounts of abstraction and higher cognition.
