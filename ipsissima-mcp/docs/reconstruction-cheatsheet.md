# Reconstructing an argument

A reconstruction is a **claim about someone else's reasoning**: that this is what they argued,
and this is how the parts hang together. It can be wrong, and it is wrong in two directions.

- **Too little**: the reconstruction misses what the text argues — leaves out a load-bearing step,
  flattens a distinction, files an objection under the wrong claim.
- **Too much**: the reconstruction supplies reasoning the author did not give, and attributes it
  to them. A tidier argument than the one on the page is not a better reconstruction of it.

Everything below is aimed at those two failures. The method is Alec Fisher's; the structural
distinctions are Trudy Govier's; both are named where used.

---

## 1. The method

### Step 0. Is this an argument at all?

A passage is an argument **if and only if** something in it is being *supported* by something
else. Not everything that looks like reasoning is.

| this is not an argument | what it is |
|---|---|
| *"The window was shut all summer and the weather was damp, so the room smelled musty."* | an **explanation** — it assumes the room smelled musty and says why |
| *"First I read Kant, then Hegel, then I gave up."* | a **narrative** |
| *"There are three views in the literature: A, B and C."* | a **report** |
| *"Consider a case where…"* | a **set-up** — the argument comes after |

**The test.** In an argument, the claims are offered as *grounds to accept* the conclusion. In an
explanation, the conclusion is *already accepted* and the claims say how it came about (Govier
ch. 1). "Because", "so", "therefore" and "thus" appear in both, so the indicator words do not
settle it — ask what the author wants you to *do* with the claim.

> Reconstructing an explanation as an argument is one of the commonest ways a map misreports a
> paper: it turns an account of how a view arose into a case for accepting it.

**Fisher's Principle of Charity, in its useful form**: if reading a passage as reasoning yields
only bad arguments, assume it is not reasoning.

### Step 1. Find the conclusion before anything else

Not the topic. Not the title. **The claim the author wants you to accept by the end.** Write it
down in your own words before reading again.

Look for conclusion indicators — *therefore, hence, thus, so, it follows that, which shows that,
I conclude that* — and reason indicators — *because, since, for, firstly… secondly, follows from
the fact that*. They are markers, not proof; each needs judgement.

A long text has a **main conclusion** and **intermediate conclusions** — claims that are both
concluded from what precedes and used as reasons for what follows. Find the main one first, then
work down. Working forwards through the sections instead produces a map of the table of contents.

**A paper may have more than one main conclusion, and forcing it to have one is a misreport.**
Two tests distinguish a second conclusion from an intermediate one:

- an intermediate conclusion is *used* — something later in the paper rests on it;
- a second main conclusion is **argued for separately and rests on nothing further**.

Where a paper argues both a thesis and, say, that a whole area has been neglected, those are two
conclusions with their own support, and the reconstruction should carry both. Joining them into
one conjunction claims the author asserted a single thing they never stated; picking one and
demoting the other silently drops half the paper's work.

What is *not* a second conclusion: a restatement in different words (that is one claim, and
Argdown's equivalence classes are what express it), or a claim that simply has nothing attached
because its relations were never drawn.

### Step 2. Work backwards, and use the Assertibility Question

For the conclusion, ask: **what immediate reasons does the text give for accepting this?** Then
ask the same of each of those reasons, until you reach claims the text simply asserts.

Where the author's intention is not clear from indicators or context, Fisher's central tool:

> **The Assertibility Question (AQ)**
> *What argument or evidence would justify me in asserting this conclusion? What would I have to
> know or believe to be justified in accepting it?*
>
> Then look to see whether the author **asserts or clearly assumes those same claims**.
>
> - If they do — it is reasonable to read them as having intended that argument.
> - **If they do not — you have no rational way of reconstructing their argument from the text.**

That last clause is the discipline. The AQ is not permission to invent the best argument in the
vicinity; it is a way of *generating candidates that you then check against the text*. A candidate
the text does not support is a candidate you drop, not one you attribute.

### Step 3. Make each claim stand alone

Each premise and conclusion should be a **complete statement** that could be true or false on its
own (Govier's standardising rules, ch. 2):

- No pronouns pointing outside the claim — not *"it is unjust"* but *"differential pricing is
  unjust"*.
- Statements, not questions, commands or exclamations. *"Don't trust the experts!"* becomes
  *"We should not trust the experts."*
- Strip the author's throat-clearing: *"it has always amazed me that"*, *"in my humble opinion"*,
  *"I am concerned that"*. These say how the author feels, not what they claim.
- **One claim per statement.** If a claim contains its own "because", split it: the inner
  reasoning is a sub-argument and should be drawn as one.
- **Keep the author's scope and hedges.** *"Most"* is not *"all"*; *"probably"* is not *"is"*; a
  conditional conclusion is not a categorical one. This is where reconstructions most often
  overreach, and it is a misreport even when every word is quoted.

### Step 4. Fix the structure

Decide, for each set of reasons, how they bear on what they support. This is the decision that
changes what the reconstruction *claims*, and it is not cosmetic.

**Linked** — the reasons work only together. Remove one and the rest support nothing.

```
(1) Athletes get plenty of exercise.
(2) Exercise keeps a person healthy.
(3) Healthy people live a long life.
-----
(4) Athletes live a long life.
```

**Convergent** — each reason bears on the conclusion independently. Remove one and the others are
untouched.

```
(1) She never takes her eyes off him in a crowd.
(2) She is restless when he is out of town.
(3) She brings his name into every conversation.
-----
(4) She is in love with him.
```

> **The test**: take one reason away. If the others still support the conclusion, they are
> convergent. If they now support nothing, they are linked.
>
> Drawing convergent reasons as linked claims the author's case collapses if any one fails —
> usually false, and unfair. Drawing linked reasons as convergent claims each premise supports
> the conclusion alone — usually false, and flattering. **When the author's intention is genuinely
> unclear, Fisher's rule: choose the reading that yields the better argument.**

Two further shapes worth naming: **serial**, where a reason supports an intermediate conclusion
which supports the main one; and **divergent**, where one premise supports two distinct
conclusions.

### Step 5. Decide what to do about what is not said

Real arguments leave premises unstated. An unstated premise is a **logical gap you can see**: the
step from these reasons to that conclusion needs something more.

Supply one **only** when all of these hold:

1. The step genuinely requires it — without it the inference does not go through.
2. The author would evidently accept it. Common knowledge and the author's own commitments
   elsewhere both count.
3. Supplying it does not make the argument better than the author's. If your addition rescues a
   bad argument, you have written a new one.

**Mark every one as yours.** An unstated premise is the reconstructor's contribution, and a reader
must be able to tell it from what the author wrote. In Ipsissima this is `fidelity: "imputation"`
with a `warrant`.

### Step 6. Place the objections, and get their kind right

Objections in a text are not all the same thing, and the difference decides how they are drawn.

**Whose objection is it?**

- A **counterconsideration** is the author's own: something they grant counts against their
  conclusion, and argue is outweighed. Marked in prose by *although, even though, despite the fact
  that, while granting that, admitting that*. Govier's point (ch. 12) is easy to lose: **a
  counterconsideration is not a premise.** The author is not offering it as support; they are
  conceding it. Draw it as an attack, and record that it is the author's.
- A **critic's objection** is someone else's, reported by the author — usually to answer it.
- **The reconstructor's objection** is yours. It may be worth recording; it must be marked as
  yours and never attributed to the author.

**What does the objection deny?** This is the distinction most often lost, and it is the one
Argdown draws with a separate relation:

| the objection says | relation |
|---|---|
| *"that premise is false"* | **attack** on the premise |
| *"that conclusion is false"* | **attack** on the conclusion |
| *"even granting all that, it does not follow"* | **undercut** on the argument |
| *"these two cannot both be true"* | **contradiction** |

An **undercut** grants the premises and denies that they license the conclusion. In the source it
sounds like: *"even if that were so, it would not show…"*, *"that may be true, but it does not
follow that…"*, *"the analogy breaks down at exactly the point that matters"*, *"this holds only
under conditions that do not obtain here"*.

> Philosophical objections are undercuts far more often than reconstructions record. Drawing an
> undercut as an attack on a premise says the author asserted something false, when the objection
> granted the premise and challenged the reasoning. That is a misreport of both parties.

### Step 7. Watch for supposition

Not every claim in an argument is **asserted**. A claim inside *"suppose that…"*, *"assume for the
sake of argument…"*, or the antecedent of a conditional is entertained, not endorsed (Fisher
ch. 8).

This matters most in a **reductio**: the author asserts P *in order to* derive something
unacceptable and conclude not-P. A reconstruction that records P as the author's own claim has
them asserting exactly what they set out to refute.

The same applies to a whole conditional. *"If people have desires nothing in this world satisfies,
they were probably made for another"* asserts the conditional, **not** its consequent — even
where the author believes the consequent on other grounds.

## 2. Charity, and where it stops

Three positions, and they converge:

- **Fisher**: if reading a passage as reasoning yields only bad arguments, it probably is not
  reasoning. Charity as a diagnostic.
- **Govier's Modest Charity**: presume people are giving reasons for claims they genuinely
  believe — but *"charity can lead us away from accuracy if it is taken too far"*. Do not improve
  someone's argument until it is no longer theirs.
- **Stern**: "the principle of charity" names no single thing, and an accurate quotation can
  misreport. Below.

**The operative rule.** Charity governs how you read an *unclear* text. It never licenses
attributing a claim the text does not make. When the choice is between a charitable reading and an
accurate one, the reconstruction records the accurate one and says what is unclear.

### 2.1 Two goals, and when they conflict

There are two things a reading of a text can be for (Stern, 287–8):

- **fit** — the meaning of the text, by the standards of the author or their contemporaries. The
  test: *would the author recognise these thoughts as her own?*
- **appropriation** — a contribution to a current debate. Getting something right, using the text.

Neither is bad, and they often coincide. But sometimes you must choose, and **the trouble is that
charity looks like a bridge between them** — as if the philosophically best reading were, for that
reason, what the text means. It is not a bridge, because it is not one principle.

### 2.2 Charity is ambiguous in three ways, and they are choices you make

Stern's argument: "the more charitable reading" is ambiguous along three dimensions, and until you
say which you mean, the phrase disguises a commitment rather than stating one. Ipsissima records
all three in front matter, so that "this reading is uncharitable" becomes something a reader can
argue with.

**`unit` — what is being chosen between.** Two quite different things:

| `unit:` | the question |
|---|---|
| `meaning` | one sentence, two readings. Did *"conscious deliberations do not cause actions"* mean *rarely* or *never*? A question of **disambiguation** |
| `commitment` | the author says P in one place and not-P in another, both unambiguously. Which did they **hold**? *He said it, but he didn't really mean it* |

**`mode` — what "best light" means.** The literature does not agree, and the candidates come
apart:

| `mode:` | best means |
|---|---|
| `coherence` | consistent with the author's other commitments |
| `truth` | not false |
| `agreement` | close to what the interpreter takes to be true or reasonable |
| `soundness` | the argument works, and fits the author's other texts |
| `interest` | true *and* not tame or boring |

> **Stern's example.** A seventeenth-century witness says the accused rode a broom to a sabbat.
> Belief in witches was supported by the leading science, history and theology of the day — so
> `coherence` says the charitable reading is that she really believed it. But there are no
> witches, and some accusers must have suspected as much — so `truth` says the charitable reading
> is that she did not. **Coherence and truth pull opposite ways, and both are called charity.**

**`strength` — how much better than their words the author is assumed to be.**

Lessing on Aristotle: *"Aristotle is not often guilty of a palpable contradiction. Where I would
seem to find one in such a man I prefer rather to mistrust my own reason."* — *in such a man*, but
not in others. On that view strength is earned, and varies by author.

The alternative is uniform strength, and then it has to be set somewhere:

| `strength:` | |
|---|---|
| `minimal` | what any minimally rational person might hold. Useless in philosophy: as long as *somebody* could hold the worse view, charity cannot choose |
| `ordinary` | what philosophers can generally be assumed to be committed to |
| `strong` | the best a trained philosopher could read them as meaning. Severs the link with ordinary meaning — nobody means that |

### 2.3 The move to watch: manufacturing openness

The natural defence of charity is that it only operates when there are *two equally plausible
interpretations*. Stern's sharpest point is that this defence fails, because **openness can be
manufactured by the very failure charity is invoked to fix**.

Suppose the author states, unambiguously, something false or inconsistent. The interpreter can
still say:

- they were being **hyperbolic** — not their considered position;
- it was **sloppy phrasing** of a different claim;
- it was a **secret sign** to knowing readers.

Each of these reopens a text that was closed. And the reasoning runs backwards: *"**because** this
is false or implausible, it is open that it is merely hyperbole."* Cause mistaken for effect.

> **This is where Ipsissima's `warrant:` field comes from, and why it exists.** `hyperbole`,
> `sloppy-phrasing` and `secret-sign` are Stern's three devices by name. Recording one is not
> forbidden — sometimes the reading is right. What is forbidden is reaching for one *without
> saying so*, because then the pattern is invisible. Three claims read as hyperbole is a decision
> about the author, and nobody notices making it one claim at a time.

A related cost: **charity rewards ambiguous writing.** Of two papers, one admitting a single
reading and one admitting a hundred, charity ensures the second comes out better — one of its
hundred readings will beat the first paper's one, and offering any of the other ninety-nine would
be uncharitable. It also forecloses the question worth asking: *why did the author write
ambiguously?*

### 2.4 Misreporting, and why it is charity's shadow

Misreporting is using an author's words to make it seem they are saying something they are
certainly not. We all reject it. Stern's uncomfortable observation is that it and charity share a
structure:

1. both can resolve a tension between the correct meaning and the best philosophy;
2. both **advertise** a commitment to meaning through a recognised, meaning-seeking technique —
   direct quotation in the one case, an interpretative "principle" in the other;
3. and both can end up **sacrificing** the very commitment they advertise, leaving more room for
   the best philosophy.

Three of Stern's four illustrations of misreporting quote **verbatim**: a hedged claim quoted
while the author's own correction, in the same sentence, is left just outside the quotation marks;
a *some* quoted in support of an *all*; a passage cited as evidence for a term it never uses.

**This is why verifying a quotation is not enough**, and why `check_argdown` reports, for every
quotation that *is* exact, what it was cut away from. A checker can verify the currency. It cannot
see the debasement, because the debasement is entirely a matter of what the span was cut away
**from**.

## 3. Forms worth recognising

Naming the form gets the skeleton right. It shapes the wiring; it is not recorded anywhere.

| form | skeleton | where objections usually land |
|---|---|---|
| **elimination of alternatives** | the candidates are X, Y, Z; none survives; so not-P | that the list is exhaustive — almost never argued |
| **refutation by counterexample** | the definition says P; here is a case where P holds and the thing does not | whether the case is really as described |
| **reductio ad absurdum** | assume P; derive something unacceptable; so not-P | whether the derived claim is really unacceptable |
| **inference to the best explanation** | E is puzzling; H explains it better than rivals; so H | whether a rival was overlooked — an **undercut** |
| **argument from analogy** | S and T are alike in these ways; S has Q; so T has Q | whether the similarities are the *relevant* ones — an **undercut** |
| **conductive / balance of considerations** | several independent factors, weighed against counterconsiderations | the weighing, not any single factor |
| **dilemma** | either P or Q; both are bad | whether the disjunction is exhaustive |
| **transcendental** | X is possible only if Y; X is actual; so Y | the necessity claim |

**A text of any length uses several forms.** Naming one at the top describes the top-level move
and usually none of the arguments inside it. Name a form for each argument you reconstruct.

**Each form has its own standard objections** — what Douglas Walton calls *critical questions*.
Knowing the form tells you what to look for in the text, and knowing which critical question an
objection answers tells you whether it is an attack or an undercut. An objection that a rival
explanation was overlooked does not deny any premise of an IBE; it denies that the premises
license the conclusion.

## 4. Conductive arguments deserve their own note

Much philosophical argument — and nearly all practical and ethical argument — is **conductive**:
several considerations, each independently relevant, weighed together, against
counterconsiderations the author acknowledges.

It is worth naming because it is easy to reconstruct badly:

- The premises are **convergent**, never linked. Each counts separately.
- The conclusion is **not entailed**. Recording a conductive argument as if its premises entailed
  its conclusion overstates what the author claimed.
- The **counterconsiderations are part of the argument**, not objections to it. An author who
  writes *"although X, still P"* has built X into their case. Leaving X out makes the argument
  look stronger and less honest than it is.
- The real action is in the **weighing**, which is usually the least explicit part of the text and
  the place a reader most needs help.

## 5. What a good reconstruction shows

1. **The main conclusion is the paper's, and is at the top.**
2. **Every claim is one claim**, stated so it could be true or false on its own.
3. **The structure is the argument's**, not the text's running order.
4. **Linked and convergent are distinguished**, and each is the author's own arrangement.
5. **Objections are the right kind** — attack, undercut or contradiction — and attributed to
   whoever made them.
6. **What the author did not say is marked as not theirs**, with a reason for supplying it.
7. **The author's hedges and scope survive.**
8. **Every claim can be found in the source**, or is marked as the reconstructor's.

## 6. Failure modes, in the order they occur

| symptom | what went wrong |
|---|---|
| the map mirrors the section headings | worked forwards instead of from the conclusion |
| a section of the paper has almost no claims | the argument there was reported, not reconstructed |
| every objection is an attack on a premise | undercuts not recognised |
| premises all hang as siblings | linked structure not identified |
| the argument is better than the paper's | charity over accuracy; unstated premises doing too much |
| an author asserts what they set out to refute | a supposition or reductio read as an assertion |
| a claim says *all* where the paper says *most* | scope not preserved |
| an acknowledged concession is missing | counterconsideration dropped as "not part of the argument" |
| a "because" was drawn as support | an explanation reconstructed as an argument |
