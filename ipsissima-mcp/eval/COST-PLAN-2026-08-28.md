# Making a reconstruction affordable: the plan

**The targets, set by the owner on 28 Aug 2026:** cut the **fixed cost by at least 60%** and the
**variable cost by at least 20%**, *without lowering the effort level*. From the regression in
`COST-2026-08-27.md`:

```
today      tokens  ≈  154,000  +  1.05 × map_bytes
target     tokens  ≤   61,600  +  0.84 × map_bytes
```

In whole runs, on the corpus as it stands: Darwin from 160,365 to about 65,000; Prescott-Couch
from 248,637 to about 137,000.

Effort stays at `max`, which rules out the largest lever already measured — `high` bought −16% and
made eight mechanical faults where `max` made none (`effort-testing/`). Everything below holds the
reading fixed and attacks the *procedure*.

### Where this got to, in three sentences

The free wins are in, and on a controlled re-run of Darwin they cut **tool calls by 85%** and
tokens by **31%** while producing a slightly better map. That is short of the 60% target, and §2b
shows why: **the fixed cost is max-effort reasoning, not round trips** — one turn of this run
thought for eight minutes and emitted 44,411 tokens, which no amount of call-cutting touches.
A second lever appeared that was not in the plan at all: that eight-minute turn **outlived the
five-minute prompt cache**, and the re-cache it forced is worth about **26%** of the run's
rate-weighted cost, recoverable by setting `subagentPromptCacheTtl` to `1h`.

**But read §3a before counting that 26%.** It applies to *subagents*, which is how this repository
runs reconstructions — not to the product. Ipsissima-MCP's user works in their own main
conversation, which on a Claude subscription **already gets a one-hour TTL**, so for them the
expiry is a cost never incurred rather than a saving available. It comes back for anyone on an API
key or a cloud provider, and — worst and least obvious — for a subscriber who has gone into usage
credits, where the protection is withdrawn exactly when the billing starts.

**Honest answer on the targets:** the 20% variable-cost target looks reachable and may already be
met. **The 60% fixed-cost target is not reachable at constant `max` effort by procedure alone.**
The free wins gave 31%; the cache setting gives ~26% in the buckets that need it and nothing in
the bucket the product usually sits in; and what remains underneath is reasoning, which is what
`max` buys. Reaching 60% means spending less effort somewhere, and New 3 — repairing at lower
effort than reading — is the least damaging place to spend it.

## 1. Where the 154,000 goes — *the model that turned out to be wrong*

> **Read §2b first.** This section is kept as written, because the measurement that refuted it was
> designed against it and the refutation is only legible beside the claim. Its arithmetic is
> plausible, it fits the observed totals, and it is not what is happening.

The earlier report established what the fixed cost **is not** — not the reference documents
(17,200 tokens, 11%), not the checker (about 6,000, 4%) — and that it *is* the agentic loop: 37 to
45 tool calls whatever the paper. It stopped there, because tokens per call were never measured.

Here is an arithmetic model that fits the observed totals. **It is a model, not a measurement**,
and the run in flight is the first test of it.

Each call re-sends the conversation. With prompt caching, the stable prefix is charged at the
cache-read rate — roughly a tenth — while that step's own reasoning and output are charged in
full. So for a run of `C` calls whose context averages `X` tokens:

```
run  ≈  C × (X × cache-read rate)   +   C × (reasoning + output per step)
        ^ carrying the conversation     ^ thinking and writing at each step
```

Darwin, with `C = 41`, a context averaging perhaps 20,000 tokens, and max-effort reasoning of
order 1,500 tokens a step:

```
41 × (20,000 × 0.1)  +  41 × 1,500   =   82,000 + 61,500   =   143,500
```

against 160,365 observed. The model is the right size, and its shape is the point:

> **Both terms are linear in the number of calls.** Not in the paper, not in the map, not in the
> documents — in how many times the loop goes round. Cut the calls by 60% and, to a first
> approximation, the fixed cost falls by 60%.

This also explains the variable cost, which is otherwise odd. **1.05 tokens per byte of map** is
about four times what a byte of English costs to *write* (≈0.25 tokens). The other three quarters
is the finished map sitting in context and being carried through every call that follows the
write — the checker rounds and the edits. So:

> **The variable cost is set by how many calls happen AFTER the map is written.** Darwin had about
> fourteen. Five would cut it by roughly two thirds.

Both targets are the same lever, which is why this plan has one theme.

## 2a. What the transcripts say, now that they can be read

`eval/run_cost.py`, written today, reads an agent's own transcript and reports the four usage
numbers per turn and a census of what each call was for. Two things came straight out of it, both
of which change §3.

**A transcript repeats itself, and naive summing is wildly wrong.** Each assistant turn is written
out once per content block as it streams, and every record carries the *same* usage with
`output_tokens` climbing to its final value on the last. Summing records rather than turns counted
one run's carried context as 145,730 tokens when it was 72,865 — a 2× overstatement. The tool now
groups by message id. **Any future measurement that does not do this is not measuring anything**,
and this is worth saying because the numbers look perfectly plausible either way.

**The round trip is the model TURN, not the tool call.** A turn can issue several tools at once,
and when it does they cost one lot of carried context between them, not one each. The first turns
of the run in flight went at 1.33 calls per turn. Every previous measurement in this project has
counted *calls*, and calls are the wrong denominator:

> **Batching independent calls into one turn is a saving that costs nothing and changes nothing
> about the work.** Reading the source and listing the folder are independent. So are re-reading
> two passages to fix two quotations. Nothing in the reconstructor's brief currently says so.

That was written up as item 0, ahead of everything else. **The finished run demoted it** — see
§2b: with six turns there is little left for batching to save, and the one place the run batched
hard, it lost. It remains free and worth instructing; it is no longer the lever.

**And the early split was misleading, which is worth recording.** Three turns in, the run showed
63% of tokens carrying the conversation against **1.1% thinking and writing**, and that looked
like strong confirmation of §1. The fourth turn then emitted 44,411 reasoning tokens and took the
share to 11%. **A partial run is not a small run**: the expensive turn had not happened yet, and
quoting the ratio before it did would have pointed this plan in exactly the wrong direction.

## 2b. THE RESULT, and it falsifies §1

Darwin was reconstructed again at `max` on 28 Aug 2026, into a folder holding nothing but the
source and `_quarto.yml`, with the changes in §2 in place. Measured with `run_cost.py`.

| | baseline (27 Aug) | this run | |
|---|---|---|---|
| tool calls | 41 | **6** | −85% |
| checker calls | 11 | **1** | −91% |
| distinct file states | 3 | 1 | |
| local pre-verification calls | 11 | **0** | −100% |
| calls to read the instructions | 3 | 2 | |
| calls to read the source | 4 | 1 | |
| tokens, as the harness reports them | 160,365 | **111,033** | **−31%** |
| wall clock | — | 10.4 min | |

The map is at least as good: 20 titled claims against 15, six premise-conclusion structures
against five, an **undercut the published sample does not have**, four warrants against two, the
same twelve verbatim spans, and `ok: true` with zero findings on the first and only check.

**Calls fell by 85% and tokens by 31%.** §5 named that as the falsifying observation, so:

> **The model in §1 is wrong. The fixed cost is not dominated by the number of round trips.**

### Where it actually goes

`run_cost.py` on the six turns:

| | tokens | share |
|---|---|---|
| carrying the conversation (cache reads) | 223,643 | 49.8% |
| new, and cached for later (cache writes) | 175,799 | 39.2% |
| thinking and writing | 49,387 | 11.0% |

and the per-turn table says the rest:

```
 turn       new   cached-w   cached-r    output
    1         2      32797          0       485
    2         2       7271      32797       588
    3         2       1363      40068       221
    4         2      23379      41431     44411   <- eight minutes of thinking
    5         2     109347          0       358   <- the cache had expired
    6         2       1642     109347      3324
```

**Two findings, and the second is worth more than everything else in this document.**

**1. Reasoning is the fixed cost.** Turn 4 emitted **44,411 output tokens** — reasoning, at `max`
— which is 40% of the 111k context the run ends with. It is not a round trip and no amount of call
cutting removes it. That is why 85% fewer calls bought 31% fewer tokens and not 85%: what was
removed was the cheap part. **At constant `max` effort the 60% fixed-cost target is probably not
reachable**, and this is the tradeoff the owner asked to have surfaced rather than absorbed.

**2. The prompt cache expired mid-run and cost 109,347 tokens.** Everything here is cached with a
**five-minute** TTL (`ephemeral_5m`; no 1-hour cache was used). Turn 4 thought for **8.2 minutes**,
which outlived its own cache, so turn 5 re-cached the entire context from scratch — 62% of all the
cache-writing in the run, for nothing.

Rate-weighted, this run costs about **291,500 tokens**; with the context kept cached it would be
about **215,600**, a **26% reduction from one configuration change with no effect on the map**.
See New 1 in §3 for that arithmetic in full, and for the correction it contains — a first pass at
this number said 43%, having forgotten that a longer-lived cache bills its *writes* at a higher
rate.

That is the largest lever found, and it is not in the original §3 because nobody was looking for
it. **It is settable**: subagents have their own TTL bucket, `subagentPromptCacheTtl`, and it
defaults to five minutes on every plan. Where it cannot be set the mitigation is weaker and the
same shape: keep any single turn under five minutes, which argues *against* over-batching.

### Which also corrects §2a, and item 0 with it

The run batched two calls into its first turn and went one-to-a-turn thereafter — 1.20 calls per
acting turn. Batching is still free and still worth instructing, but **it is no longer the top
item**: with six turns there is very little left for it to save, and the run's own report notes
that its one attempt at aggressive batching *backfired*. Reading the three documents and the
source in a single `cat` produced 57 KB, over the tool's inline cap, so it spilled to a file that
then had to be read back — two calls for what should have been one, and a giant turn that took
eight minutes to digest and expired the cache.

> **Over-batching is not free.** It converts several cheap turns into one long one, and a long turn
> is exactly what breaks the cache. Batch *independent, small* calls; do not batch the reading.

## 3. The plan for the rest, revised by the result above

**Reordered on 28 Aug 2026 after the Darwin run.** The old order assumed call count was the cost.
It is not, so the items that touch *reasoning volume* and *cache behaviour* come first, and the
call-cutting items — largely done, and worth their 31% — move down.

### New 1. Set the subagent cache TTL to an hour — **answered: it is settable**

**It can be set, and the default explains exactly what was observed.** From Claude Code's prompt
caching documentation: *"Subagents fall outside the main-conversation TTL bucket, so they get five
minutes even on a subscription until you choose a longer one."* A reconstructor is a subagent, so
it gets five minutes whatever the plan — which is why every cache write in the Darwin run came
back as `ephemeral_5m_input_tokens`.

| | |
|---|---|
| setting | `subagentPromptCacheTtl` |
| environment variable | `CLAUDE_CODE_SUBAGENT_PROMPT_CACHE_TTL` |
| accepted values | `5m` or `1h`; **anything else is ignored** |
| requires | Claude Code **v2.1.242 or later** |
| precedence | `FORCE_PROMPT_CACHING_5M=1` → the bucket's env var → the bucket's setting → `ENABLE_PROMPT_CACHING_1H=1` → the default |

**The arithmetic, corrected.** The first estimate here said 43%, and it was wrong: it assumed the
write rate stayed the same. It does not — a one-hour cache **bills writes at a higher rate** than a
five-minute one. On the published multipliers (5m write 1.25×, **1h write 2×**, read 0.1×), the
Darwin run measures:

| | 5m, as it ran | 1h, no expiry |
|---|---|---|
| cache writes | 175,799 × 1.25 = 219,749 | 66,452 × 2 = 132,904 |
| cache reads | 223,643 × 0.1 = 22,364 | 332,990 × 0.1 = 33,299 |
| output | 49,387 | 49,387 |
| **rate-weighted total** | **≈ 291,500** | **≈ 215,600** |

**About −26%, not −43%**, and still the largest single lever on this list, still with no effect on
the map. *(Multipliers are the published ones and are an assumption; the token counts are
measured.)*

**It is a bucket setting, not an agent setting, and that decides how to use it.** There is no
frontmatter field for cache TTL — the full subagent frontmatter list has none — so the setting
applies to *everything* in the bucket: every subagent whatever its job, workflows, teammates,
forks, **compaction**, and session titles. It cannot be pointed at reconstructions alone.

That matters because the longer TTL is not free. It buys nothing unless a run actually idles past
five minutes, and it charges more either way: **writes at 2× base instead of 1.25×**. Every short
subagent in the bucket therefore pays about a 40% premium on its cache writes for a lifetime it
never uses. Sketching a mixed session in this repository:

| | rate-weighted effect of `1h` |
|---|---|
| one reconstruction (measured: 66,452 non-expiry writes) | **saves ≈ 76,000** |
| five short search agents (≈ 30,000 writes each, *estimated*) | **costs ≈ 112,000** |

**In a session that builds one map and does a lot of ordinary searching, this is plausibly net
negative** — reconstructions are rare and expensive, quick subagents are frequent, cheap and
numerous. The short-agent figure is an estimate from the reconstructor's own write volume, not a
measurement; the direction is not in doubt. Compaction is in the bucket too, and is usually a
single request that writes once and is never read again, so it pays the premium for nothing every
time.

**So: not in settings, project settings included.** The right scope is a shell dedicated to map
rebuilds, where the bucket is nearly all long-turn work and the premium lands only on runs that
recoup it:

```bash
CLAUDE_CODE_SUBAGENT_PROMPT_CACHE_TTL=1h claude
```

Ordinary sessions stay on five minutes. **This corrects an earlier version of this passage** which
offered "the settings key in this project only" as an option: this project is not a
reconstruction-only environment, and most of its subagent use is short-lived.

`FORCE_PROMPT_CACHING_5M=1` overrides everything, for A/B comparison.

**Verify it took, don't assume.** `run_cost.py` now reports cache writes by TTL bucket, and says
so when they are all five-minute. A setting that is ignored — a mistyped value, an older Claude
Code — looks exactly like one that was never made.

### New 2. Keep any single turn under the cache TTL

Follows directly. It means: do not hand the model 57 KB and ask it to think about all of it at
once. Read the documents, then the source, as separate turns — which is what the run did *not* do,
and its eight-minute turn is the result. **This reverses the advice in §2 item 3**, which was
written before there was any evidence about turn length.

### New 3. Split the fix loop off at a lower effort *(was D)*

Now the main lever on reasoning volume, which §2b shows is the fixed cost. `max` buys the reading;
re-quoting a span the checker has already located is clerical. On this run the whole reconstruction
was one reasoning turn, so there was no fix loop to split — but on the four runs in the baseline
that needed repair rounds, those rounds carry `max`-effort reasoning for mechanical work.

### New 4. Never re-read what you just wrote *(was C)*

The run did do this: the census shows one **read the map** call. On Darwin that is 14 KB; on
Prescott-Couch it would be 89 KB of pure duplication, entering the context and then being carried
and re-cached. Cheap to forbid, and now measured.

### New 5. Batch independent calls into one turn *(was 0)*

One paragraph in `extraction-prompt.md` and one in the reconstructor's brief, naming the cases:
the source and the folder together; several passages re-read together; several edits applied
together. **Free, immediate, and it divides the round-trip count by whatever the batching factor
turns out to be.** `run_cost.py` reports calls per turn, so the next run says whether it worked.

### New 6. One call for the read phase — `ipsissima brief <folder>` *(was A)*

Even after the changes above, a run opens with: read three documents (1), read the source (1),
read the folder conventions (1). A single command that emits the extraction prompt, the three
reference documents, `_quarto.yml`, the front-matter conventions and the whole source — in one
result — makes that **one call instead of three**, and removes the last reason to look around.

*Expected:* −2 calls, ~5% of the fixed cost. *Cost:* a small script. *Risk:* none; it is `cat`
with a manifest. *Measured by:* the call census in the next run's COST section.

### New 7. A checker that returns an applicable patch *(was B)*

The fix loop today is: check → read the finding → locate the line → edit → check. Where the
checker has already worked out the correction — and for a failing quotation it has, because it
found the real text — it could return a patch the caller applies without thinking about it.

*Expected:* −1 to −2 calls per fix round; on a run with three rounds, −3 to −6 calls, and those
are **post-write** calls, so this hits the variable cost hardest. *Cost:* moderate; the checker
already computes the corrections, so this is serialisation plus an `--apply` path. *Risk:* real —
a wrong automatic edit is worse than a round trip. Must be `!`-severity mechanical fixes only,
never a `?`, and never anything touching the reading. *Measured by:* post-write call count.

### New 8. Reconsider what the three documents cost to carry *(was E)*

17,200 tokens read at step two are then carried, at cache-read rate, through every remaining call.
Under the model that is about 42% of a 40-call run's carrying cost — but only about 14% of a
15-call one, which is why this is *fifth* and not first: **fixing the loop shrinks this problem
without touching the documents.** Halving them was already costed at −6% of a run and rejected as
a quality trade, and that judgement stands.

What is worth doing is the cheap half: check whether all three are needed *before writing any
node*, or whether `ipsissima-conventions.md` (13,620 bytes, all of it about how to annotate) could
be read after the structure is settled. That is a reordering, not a cut.

## 3a. WHICH BUCKET THE PRODUCT IS IN, and why that changes New 1

*Raised by the owner, and it is the right question: everything above was measured on a
**subagent**, because that is how reconstructions are run in this repository. But Ipsissima-MCP is
an **MCP server**. Its user is in their **own main conversation**, with the prompt served by the
`prompts` primitive, the reference documents by `resources`, and the checker as a tool. That is a
different TTL bucket with a different default, and the difference runs the other way.*

| request bucket | Claude subscription, within plan usage | usage credits, API key, or cloud provider |
|---|---|---|
| **main conversation** ← the product | **one hour** | five minutes |
| everything else — subagents, forks, compaction ← this repo's dev loop | five minutes | five minutes |

### What follows for the three kinds of user

**1. On a subscription, within plan usage: the problem does not exist.** The main conversation
already gets an hour, so an eight-minute thinking turn does not expire anything. **The 26% is not
a saving available to them — it is a cost they were never paying.** The single largest event in
§2b is an artefact of how *this repository* runs reconstructions, and does not describe the
product's main path. That is worth being plain about, because it was presented as the headline.

**2. On an API key, a cloud provider, or through a gateway: they get five minutes in the main
conversation too**, and hit exactly what §2b measured. Their fix is the **main-conversation**
control, `promptCacheTtl` / `CLAUDE_CODE_PROMPT_CACHE_TTL`, **not** the subagent one. Anyone
driving Ipsissima programmatically is in this group.

**3. On a subscription that has gone into usage credits: the protection disappears exactly when
the billing starts.** Over the plan limit, Claude Code drops the main conversation to the cheaper
five-minute TTL because the user is now paying per token. A long reconstruction is precisely the
kind of work that pushes someone over. **This is the worst case and the least obvious**: the same
job that cost nothing extra last week starts expiring its cache the moment it begins to be billed,
and nothing in the interface says so. Setting `promptCacheTtl: 1h` restores it.

### What is *worse* about the main conversation, and is not about TTL at all

A subagent's context is thrown away when it finishes. **A reconstruction in the user's own
conversation is not.** Everything it touched stays: about 68 KB of reference documents, the whole
source text, the finished map, and every checker result — carried on every later turn of that
session until `/clear` or `/compact`, including turns about something else entirely.

That is a real cost the subagent model does not have, and it points the plan's remaining items in
a slightly different direction:

- **New 4 (never re-read what you just wrote) matters more here**, because the duplicate is not
  discarded at the end of a task; it is carried for the rest of the session.
- **New 8 (what the documents cost to carry) matters more here too.** In a subagent, 68 KB is
  carried for six turns and dropped. In the user's session it is carried indefinitely.
- **Compaction becomes a correctness risk, not merely a cost.** If a reconstruction pushes the
  session over its context limit, auto-compaction replaces the history with a summary — and it can
  summarise away the source text or the half-finished map *mid-job*. A subagent large enough to
  compact is rare; a user's working session is not.

### Two things the product cannot control, and must therefore document

**Effort and model belong to the user's session.** `reconstructor.md` can declare `effort: max`; an
MCP prompt cannot. A user running the same job at a lower effort gets a cheaper and, on the
evidence in `effort-testing/`, a more fault-prone reconstruction — and Ipsissima has no way to ask
for more. **The prompt should say what the job wants and why**, and leave the choice where it
actually sits.

**Switching effort mid-session invalidates the whole cache.** Each effort level is its own cache
key. So "set it to max when you start the reconstruction" is the expensive way round: the next
request re-reads the entire conversation. Set it *before* the session, or at a `/clear`.

### One design rule for the server itself

**Never mark this server's tools `alwaysLoad`, and never document a setup that does.** Deferred
tools — the default — append when a server connects, disconnects or changes its tool list, leaving
the user's cached prefix intact. Tools loaded into the prefix invalidate the whole conversation on
any of those events, and a **stdio** server (which this is) that crashes and reconnects does
exactly that, through no action of the user's. There is no `alwaysLoad` guidance in this repository
today; this is written down so that none gets added.

## 4. How each of these gets measured

Nothing here is to be believed on argument. The instruments are in place:

- **`eval/run_cost.py`** reads an agent transcript and gives model turns, tool calls, calls per
  turn, the four usage numbers and a census of what each call was for — including *reading the
  map*, which no earlier count separated. This replaces counting calls by hand out of transcripts,
  which is how the first report was built and is both slow and easy to get wrong.
- **`~/.ipsissima/check-log.jsonl`** records every check with a content hash, so checker calls
  against distinct file states is a fact, not an estimate. `eval/check_patterns.py` reads it.
- **The reconstructor's COST section** is now mandatory and its shape is fixed: total calls,
  checker calls, distinct states, instruction and source calls, tokens, wall clock. It also asks
  for rules broken and why, because a run that overspends and says so is the useful one.
- **`eval/compare_reconstructions.py`** decides whether a cheaper run produced a worse map, which
  is the question that matters more than the saving. Fidelity mix is the sharpest single number:
  the `high` arm's collapse from 50% `quotation` to 29% is what a cost saving looks like when it
  has gone too far.

**A saving that costs fidelity is not a saving**, and the pairing of those two instruments is how
this stays honest.

### The comparability problem, which has to be said plainly

The baseline figures — Darwin's 160,365, the 154,000 fixed cost, the whole regression — come from
**the agents' own reported usage**, and it is not known whether those numbers included cache
reads. `run_cost.py` measures all four components separately and can report either convention, but
it cannot retrospectively tell us which one the six baseline runs were quoting.

So **the comparable quantity across the old runs and the new ones is the call census, not the
token total.** Calls were counted the same way both times (by hand then, by tool now, and the tool
was checked against a hand count). Where this plan claims a token saving against 154,000 it is
claiming it on the model in §1, and the honest form of the claim is: *calls fell by X%, and on the
model that implies a token saving of about X%.*

Putting that right is cheap and worth doing: **re-run one paper from the baseline set and measure
it with `run_cost.py`**, so that at least one point exists on both scales. Darwin is the one being
run today, which makes it that point.

## 5. The model in §1 was wrong, and this is what it predicted

Written before the run, kept verbatim because it called the outcome:

> The alternative is that the fixed cost is dominated not by call count but by **reasoning tokens
> per step**, which are a function of effort and of how hard each step is, and which fewer calls
> would not proportionally reduce. The signature is the run in flight coming back with roughly a
> third of the calls and nowhere near a third off the tokens.
>
> Should that happen, the levers are different ones and the order reverses: **D** rises to the top
> … and the honest answer to the 60% target may be that it is not reachable at constant effort.

The run came back with **a seventh of the calls and 31% off the tokens**, which is that signature
and more extreme than the version anticipated. §3 is reordered accordingly and **D** is now New 3.

The one thing §5 did not anticipate is the cache expiry, which is not about call count *or*
reasoning volume but about how long a single turn takes — and which is worth more than either.
Writing down the falsifying observation in advance was worth doing; it did not make the guess
complete.

## 6. Not on the list

**Writing less map.** Notes are 13.3% of the corpus's bytes and verbatim `source:` spans 23.1% —
together over a third of everything written. They are also the two things that make a
reconstruction checkable rather than merely plausible. Trimming them would show up as a variable
cost saving and as a worse map, and `effort-testing/` already shows what that looks like.
